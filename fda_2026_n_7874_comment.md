## Comment text (variant B, reference sentence included)

Comment on Docket No. FDA-2026-N-7874, "Considerations for the Regulation of Generative AI-Enabled Medical Devices: Discussion Paper and Request for Feedback"

This comment responds to Questions 18, 21, 25, and 26. One suggestion, four applications: the paper's hardest problems are accountability-architecture problems, and the agency already owns the instrument that solves them. The public-welfare doctrine of United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277 (1943), and United States v. Park, 421 U.S. 658 (1975) — responsibility attaching to the natural person with practical power to prevent or correct a violation, which delegation does not relieve — was built at FDA's statutory home. The paper's own citations reach the same gap: clinicians face professional and legal consequences when standards fail, and analogous mechanisms are needed for devices (paper, n.16).

Q18 (premarket uncertainty traded for postmarket monitoring). The trade is sound only where the monitoring side is mandatory, time-boxed, and owned. Four characteristics justify it: (1) incident definitions prespecified, not discovered in litigation; (2) reporting clocks that run from when the certified monitoring program would have detected the event, so that not looking starts the clock; (3) records duties that make destruction and falsification the offense, never disclosure — a filed nonconformity should be a protected act; and (4) a named natural person answerable for the operation of the monitoring program. Absent these, premarket uncertainty is not being managed; it is being transferred to patients. Where the approach is not appropriate: the upper-right of the paper's own Figure 1 — fully autonomous action-taking where consequences are severe — because postmarket detection there is post-harm detection.

Q21 (ecosystem roles without diffusing manufacturer accountability). The question answers itself in the doctrine's terms: diffusion is prevented by non-delegable responsibility. Ecosystem participation is safe when exactly one identified natural person of the manufacturer — an officer with authority to halt the device — signs the monitoring commitments and retains responsibility that no delegation, vendor contract, committee, or consortium relieves. Everyone else — clinicians, institutions, professional societies, standards bodies, machine supervisory agents — is load-bearing help, never load-bearing responsibility. This is Park's holding operationalized: authority to prevent or correct, held by an identifiable person, is the unit that cannot be diffused. A signature requirement costs the ecosystem nothing and preserves the one address enforcement needs.

Q25 (voluntary Foundation Model MAFs; limited disclosure incentives). The paper names the defect itself: developers "may have limited incentive to disclose safety-relevant information," and voluntary, confidential, consequence-free filing is the pre-1906 architecture. Two cures are within reach. First, the signature: any MAF should be attested by an identified responsible officer of the model developer. A submission to FDA already carries 18 U.S.C. § 1001 exposure; attestation converts a courtesy into a statement with an owner, at no new statutory cost. Second, the alternative mechanism Question 25 requests already exists: California Business and Professions Code § 22757.12, New York General Business Law § 1421, and Illinois P.A. 104-0538 § 10 now impose transparency and safety-framework duties on frontier developers as obligations, not favors. Aligning MAF content (paper, n.24) with those enacted duties would let one document discharge both and premarket review receive as reference what state law already compels.

Q26 (agentic devices). Make the deployed configuration, not the model, the unit of evaluation — the paper's own instinct in Section V.A, carried to the agentic case. Concretely: an evaluation attaches to an identified model version together with the tools, memory, retrieval, credentials, and permissions attached; a device evaluated without tools is evaluated for no configuration granting external access; and the grant of a new class of tools, credentials, or permissions — a change that moves the device rightward on Figure 1's own activity axis — is itself a triggering event for re-benchmarking under element A.1 — alongside human-oversight checkpoints before irreversible actions and injection resistance across user inputs, retrieved content, and tool outputs — the failure surfaces Appendix A lists. The acceptance criterion Question 26 seeks is the configuration manifest: what the agent can reach is what was evaluated, and nothing more.

These four answers are one answer. The paper asks how novel machinery can be overseen; the reply is the agency's oldest: a duty, a record, a clock, and a name.

A public-domain research draft developing this architecture in statutory form is published at github.com/llmaolaw/model-act. It is offered as reference, not authority.
